Hi! My name is Ailing Jin. I am a PhD candidate in the Department of Geography, Sustainability, Community, and Urban Studies at the University of Connecticut, expecting to graduate in December 2026.

My research lies at the intersection of Geographic Information Science (GIS), environmental exposure, and public health. I apply advanced GIS, geospatial data analysis, and environmental sensing to develop innovative methods for personal air pollution exposure and health risk assessment.

My work advances geospatial approaches to exposure assessment by integrating emerging sensing technologies with fine-scale spatiotemporal models, moving beyond traditional static, outdoor-focused, and concentration-based frameworks. Furthermore, my research narrows the geographic scale of exposure assessment from city-wide mobility to the micro-geographic scale of indoor mobility within rooms to capture exposure variation at finer spatial scales.

My dissertation is titled Fine-Scale Assessment of Personal Exposure to Indoor and Outdoor Air Pollution Using Geospatial and Real-Time Sensing Technologies.

      Research

      My research develops spatiotemporal methods that link where people are, what they breathe, and how much they inhale, across scales ranging from city-wide daily mobility to individual rooms within a home. I integrate sensor networks, GPS and indoor positioning, spatiotemporal statistical modeling, and geovisualization.

      Modeling Spatiotemporal Variations of Indoor Air Pollution with Sensor Networks

      • Spatiotemporal modeling of indoor pollutant transport
      • Door-adjusted dynamic indoor distance
      • Spatiotemporal dependence
      • Indoor air pollution transport mapping
      • Deployed networks of low-cost sensors across rooms in residential settings to monitor cooking-generated PM2.5 in real time, along with door status.
      • Developed a barrier-aware indoor distance measure that accounts for walls, doorways, and whether doors are open or closed.
      • Built spatiotemporal models of PM2.5 propagation time and room-level concentration that explained 86% of the variation and accurately predicted concentrations at held-out monitors (r = 0.91), showing that indoor spatial structure and door status shape room-level exposure.

      Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE)-Based Room-Level Indoor Exposure Assessment

      • Indoor positioning
      • Sensor networks
      • Multi-source data integration
      • Mobility-based exposure assessment
      • Develop a BLE-based indoor sensing framework for assessing air pollution exposure based on people's mobility within the home.
      • Combine room-level air quality monitoring with low-burden indoor localization to estimate personal indoor exposure at fine spatial and temporal scales.
      • Advance methods for exposure assessment at the building scale.

      Mobility- and Inhaled Dose-Based PM2.5 Exposure and Health Risk Assessment

      • Multi-source data integration
      • 3D space-time paths
      • Group differences in exposure and health risk
      • Integrated GPS-enabled personal PM2.5 monitoring, real-time physical activity (PA) intensity, and physiological data from 50 participants in Beijing to estimate mobility- and PA-based inhaled dose.
      • Compared inhaled dose with conventional residence- and concentration-based metrics in identifying group differences in exposure and non-carcinogenic risk.
      • Gender differences emerged only in inhaled dose (higher among men), while lower educational attainment was linked to higher exposure and risk in all mobility-based metrics but not in the residence-based metric.
      • 3D space-time geovisualization showed that inhaled dose did not always track exposure concentration, highlighting the mediating role of PA and providing empirical evidence of the uncertain geographic context problem (UGCoP) in health risk assessment.

      Assessing Restaurant-Visit Patterns in the Greater Hartford Region Using Mobile Phone Location Data and Socioeconomic Factors

      • Large-scale mobility data
      • Social Vulnerability Index (SVI)
      • Community-level data analysis
      • Socioeconomic disparities in food environments
      • Leveraged large-scale GPS-based mobile phone location data to characterize restaurant patronage patterns across 396 restaurants in Greater Hartford, Connecticut.
      • Found that only 8.4% of customers visited restaurants within their own census tract, challenging residence-based definitions of community food environments and providing empirical evidence of selective daily mobility bias.
      • Restaurants in socially vulnerable areas attracted more total visits, a larger share of local customers, and more visitors from other vulnerable areas, underscoring the need to incorporate human mobility into food environment research.
